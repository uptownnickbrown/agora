Live order-book markets

→ Supply & demand, equilibrium

Every good trades on a continuous double auction against real classmates. When the Festival spikes demand, students watch the shortage, the spike, and the glut play out in their own data.

Price decrees

→ Price controls & deadweight loss

Drought sends bread soaring; the Crown caps the price; the shelves empty by nightfall. Students feel who bears the cost of a ceiling before they ever see the welfare triangle.

Crown license auctions

→ Monopoly, entry, antitrust

Glowdye requires a Crown license, auctioned to a handful of students. They price like monopolists — until the Second Charter mints more licenses and entry does what entry does.

The fishery commons

→ Externalities & common resources

An open-access fishery will collapse, because intro students do exactly what theory predicts. The recovery — quotas or tradable permits — is the rest of the lesson.

Production facilities

→ Cost curves & the firm

Mills and looms have fixed build costs, variable upkeep, and workers with genuinely diminishing returns. Students pick factory or artisan shop, then live with their break-even math.

Defensible by design

Assessment you can put in a syllabus

Every objective is written to a Bloom level, every item maps to an objective, and every mastery number traces back to answers you can read.

Evaluate

"Decide whether to take one more unit of an action by comparing its marginal benefit to its marginal cost, setting sunk costs aside."

Analyze

"Explain why open access leads each user to overuse a shared resource even while the group is worse off, and evaluate fixes such as quotas, permits, and ownership."

30 objectives adapted from OpenStax Ch. 2–13, each rewritten as an observable, Bloom-aligned behavior — not a topic label — from Understand-level on-ramps to Evaluate-level judgment calls. Students see these full statements (and their own mastery meter for each) in Pip's Study; your heatmap shows the same objectives as columns you can click into.

The actual items, not a promise of items

Four from the bank of 171 — definitional on-ramps, misconception-anchored multiple choice, diagram reads, and rubric-graded free response with a follow-up loop.

UnderstandOpportunity cost · week 1

The opportunity cost of a choice is:

The value of the single next-best alternative you gave up
The value of ALL the alternatives you gave up, added together
The coins you spent on it
The effort it took

Every objective has a definitional on-ramp like this, and every item carries a written explanation students read after answering — why the right answer is right, and why the tempting one tempts.

ApplyMarginal thinking · week 1

A caravan pays 60c each for up to 5 tools. Your first three cost 40c of inputs apiece; rushing two more would cost 70c apiece. How many do you sell?

3 — sell while the marginal 60c beats the marginal cost, stop when it doesn't
5 — a sale is a sale
0 — rush work is never worth it
4 — split the difference

Each distractor is a named misconception: revenue-chasing, loss aversion, anchoring.

AnalyzePrice controls · week 3

The Crown pins the price at the ceiling line. Reading the chart at that price:

A shortage: buyers want 70 units but sellers bring only 30

Students read the same painted diagrams in the game; 15 items are chart reads like this one.

UnderstandDeadweight loss · week 3

The Bread Decree capped prices "to help buyers." In a sentence or two, explain how buyers themselves can end up worse off.

Grading rubric (LLM-graded, keyword fallback): credit shortage at any legal price, queueing and rationing costs, gray markets, blocked trades destroying value buyers would have enjoyed.

Free responses arrive right after the student lived it — and grading isn't the end: students can refine their answer or ask why, and get re-graded on the exchange. Growth counts.

Every objective carries at least five hand-written items. When a student practices past the bank, Pip writes a fresh one on the spot — grounded in the objective, that chapter's OpenStax text, and the questions already asked — and every generated item is stored verbatim for your review.

1

Embedded tutor checks

Pip brings one check to every student every day, picked from what's happening in their own market — and opens it for them, so the shy ones aren't skipped. Two to four minutes: MCQs, diagram reads, and one-sentence free responses graded by rubric.

2

Mastery, not memory

Each objective carries a rolling, recency-weighted mastery estimate, and wrong-then-right counts as growth: students can refine a graded answer in a follow-up exchange and be re-graded. You see the whole class as a heatmap; students see their own meters in Pip's Study — so red cells tend to heal between lectures.

3

Participation ≠ wealth

Grades reward session regularity, market activity, event participation, and demonstrated understanding, never coin balance. The student who got rich cornering wool and the student who went bankrupt learning why are graded on the same evidence: what they understood.

4

Integrity by design

Dozens of low-stakes checks make the return on cheating terrible, because there's no single exam worth outsourcing. The anomaly engine also watches for multi-accounting, collusion, and suspicious wealth transfers, using the same machinery that detects cartels.

Why they log in without being told to

Learning that doesn't feel like homework

Students log in for the fun, stay for the economy, and learn because the pedagogy is woven into both.

The tutor

Professor Pip, market pigeon

A know-it-all pigeon backed by a frontier LLM. Pip answers questions Socratically, with the student's own holdings and market data in view, and brings one question to every student every day — multiple choice, charts to read, or a sentence to write. In his Study, students watch their mastery meters fill and drill any objective that wobbles. He never plays the game for them.

"Coo! Your bread sold out the moment the Decree hit. Curious, isn't it? The price said one thing and the empty shelf said another. Which one was telling the truth?"

Common Threads

A Connections-style daily puzzle: sixteen tiles, four hidden groups, one board for the whole class. The categories are the course. Two minutes of vocabulary review students genuinely fight over.

Fishing at the Docks

Cast, wait through the nibbles, strike when the bobber dives. Named species, personal bests, mounted trophies — and the on-ramp to Week 6, because whoever loves the Docks most feels the commons collapse hardest.

The haggling caravan

Once a day, a traveler with a hidden reservation price and three chances to strike a deal. Consumer and producer surplus, taught by someone who has never lost a negotiation.

The Agora Crier

The town newspaper covers overnight price moves, named movers, and — during Market Wars — investigative reporting that makes cartel strategy uncomfortably public. Students check it the way they check a group chat.

Fair questions

What colleagues ask first

How much of my time does this really take?

We target under 30 minutes a week, and you never have to log in. The Monday Brief lands in your inbox with the week's playbook, class mastery, students who need a nudge, and the gradebook as CSV. The seven-week arc runs on autopilot, and the dashboard is there when you want to reach for a lever.

Can students cheat?

The economics is multiplayer, so there is no answer key to copy. Checks are frequent and low-stakes, so the ROI on cheating any single one is terrible by design. Grades reward engagement and demonstrated understanding, never wealth, and the anomaly system watches for multi-accounting and suspicious transfers.

What if some students just won't play?

Agora is asynchronous-first: students play 20–60 minutes a few times a week, nothing is scheduled, and 48 hours offline can't ruin anyone (soft bankruptcy is a fresh-start loan). Disengagement shows up on your dashboard before it becomes a grade problem, and the daily puzzle, streak chests, the haggling caravan, and fishing exist precisely to make logging in the path of least resistance.

How do grades get to my LMS?

Grades combine participation and mastery with weights you control, and they export as CSV today. The grade model is already LTI-1.3-shaped, and LTI delivery is the first fast-follow.

What does it cost students?

Nothing in the pilot. It pairs with the free OpenStax text (CC BY), runs in any browser including phones, and there is no real money anywhere in the game; coins are coins.

Does it work for a small section?

NPC liquidity auto-scales with roster size. Below ~12 students some phenomena (cartel formation, the fishery collapse) get an NPC assist so the story beats still land, and you'll see a gentle "thin world" note rather than a broken week.
