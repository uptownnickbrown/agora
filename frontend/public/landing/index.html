Your students live inside a working economy — and the economics that emerges is real.

Agora is a multiplayer market-town simulation that replaces the textbook-and-problem-set experience in introductory microeconomics. Prices come from your students' actual order flow. Shortages, monopolies, and collapsing commons happen because they made them happen. You formalize in lecture what they just lived.

No filler game

Every mechanic is a syllabus concept

Agora has no grinding, no combat, no busywork systems. Each major mechanic exists because a chapter of intro micro needs it — and students meet the concept by using the mechanic, days before you name it in lecture.

Live order-book markets

→ Supply & demand, equilibrium

Every good trades on a continuous double auction with real bids and asks from real classmates. Price charts are first-class UI. When the Lantern Festival spikes demand for garments, students watch the shortage, the spike, the supply response, and the glut — in their own data.

Price decrees

→ Price controls & deadweight loss

When drought sends bread soaring, the Crown caps its price at the old level. Shelves empty. A gray market may emerge — we let it. Students feel who actually bears the cost of a ceiling before they ever see the welfare triangle.

Crown license auctions

→ Monopoly, entry, antitrust

Glowdye can only be made under a Crown license, sold by sealed-bid auction to a handful of students. License holders price like monopolists — until the Second Charter announces more licenses and entry compresses margins toward the competitive outcome.

The fishery commons

→ Externalities & common resources

The Docks fishery regenerates logistically under open access — which means it will collapse, because intro students do exactly what theory predicts. The recovery, via quotas or tradable permits, is the rest of the lesson.

Production facilities

→ Cost curves & the firm

Mills, looms, and smithies have build costs (fixed), per-cycle inputs and upkeep (variable), and hired workers with genuinely diminishing returns. Students choose between high-fixed factories and low-fixed artisan shops — then live with their break-even math.

The seven-week arc

One persistent class economy, seven weeks of scripted world events — each engineered so a textbook phenomenon emerges from your students' own decisions. Weeks unlock on your pacing.

Week 1 · Ch. 1–2

The Traveling Merchant

Trading only, no production — with deliberately unbalanced endowments, so trade is obviously valuable. A comparative-advantage routing puzzle funds each student's first shop.

The Bread Decree

The Charter Choice

Guild charter factory (high fixed cost, low marginal) or artisan shop (low fixed, high marginal)? You swing demand down, then up, and students journal predict-then-observe against their own break-even point — while hiring workers at a wage with diminishing returns.

Week 5 · Ch. 8–9

The Glowdye Licenses

The Crown auctions 4–6 exclusive glowdye licenses by sealed bid. License holders meet posted demand like little monopolists while everyone else trades in thick competitive markets — then the Second Charter announces more licenses, and entry does what entry does.

Gray Skies

Heavy production now emits smog: the art literally desaturates as it accumulates, efficiency drops district-wide, and luxury demand sags. The resolution is a Pigouvian soot levy — scrubbers are for sale, and suddenly someone buys one.

Week 6 · Ch. 13

The Fishery Collapse

Open access meets a regenerating stock, and the stock loses. Students who fished for fun since week two watch yields wither — then debate quotas versus tradable permits versus property rights, with skin in the game.

A four-day team capstone. Compacts — formal agreements with visible terms and zero enforcement — make cartels possible and defection irresistible. Cartels form, price wars erupt, the Crier reports it all, and your antitrust lever is one click away.

Low-touch god mode

You don't play the game. You run the world.

Everything important is push-based, and every lever is one click. Target instructor commitment: under 30 minutes a week — most of it reading a report we wrote for you.

  • The five-minute dashboard

    World vitals at a glance — active students, price charts, wealth Gini, volumes, smog and fishery health — plus a feed of detected moments: price spikes, hoarding, cartel signatures, gray markets, disengaged students.

  • One-click diegetic interventions

    Sixteen kinds — droughts, festivals, ceilings, floors, Pigouvian taxes, licenses, antitrust suits, quotas — each previewable and schedulable. Students never see a settings change; they see the Crier announce that the Royal Granary has spoken.

  • The Monday-morning Lecture Playbook

    Each week: what happened in your class's economy (with charts), 3–5 discussion questions keyed to decisions real students actually made, misconceptions the tutor detected, and suggested next-week interventions. Slides-friendly export.

  • Mastery heatmap & gradebook

    Learning-objective × student heatmap, engagement flags, configurable grade weights, CSV export. The grade model is LTI-shaped from day one; LTI 1.3 delivery is a fast-follow.

Students log in for the fun layer, stay for the economy, and learn because the pedagogy is woven into both. Here's the part they'll tell their roommates about.

The tutor

Professor Pip, market pigeon

A know-it-all pigeon with a head for prices, backed by a frontier LLM. Pip answers questions Socratically, in character, with the student's own holdings and market data in view. He nudges, he quizzes, he celebrates — and he never plays the game for them: no trading on their behalf, no leaking hidden information, no financial advice that does their thinking.

"Coo! Your bread sold out the moment the Decree hit. Curious, isn't it — the price said one thing, the empty shelf said another. Which one was telling the truth?"

Market Mastermind

A two-minute daily deduction puzzle — same for the whole class, with Wordle-style heat feedback, shareable emoji result cards, and streak leaderboards. Deducing a mystery equilibrium price is the hook; supply-and-demand reasoning is the trick.

Fishing at the Docks

A timing minigame that yields fish, coppers, and rare mounted trophies — from the Smug Trout to the Gilded Leviathan. It's also the on-ramp to Week 6: the students who love the Docks most feel the commons collapse hardest.

Cosmetics & bragging rights

Earned prestige cosmetics, a coin-priced Luxury Boutique, shop decoration, achievement titles — "Cloth Baron," "Arbitrage Artist," "Survived the Drought" — and leaderboards that reward cleverness, not grinding.

The Agora Crier

The town newspaper, published at every market close: overnight price moves, named movers, event coverage, and — during Market Wars — investigative reporting that makes cartel strategy uncomfortably public. Students check it the way they check a group chat.

Defensible by design

Assessment you can put in a syllabus

Engagement is lovely; evidence is better. Agora's grade model was designed to survive a curriculum-committee meeting.

1

Embedded tutor checks

2–4 minute checks — MCQs, graph reads, predict-then-observe, one-sentence free responses graded by rubric — delivered in character at the teachable moment, roughly 8–12 per student per week. Every item is tagged to a learning objective adapted from OpenStax Ch. 2–13.

2

Mastery, not memory

Each of 29 learning objectives carries a rolling, recency-weighted mastery estimate. Wrong-then-right counts as growth. You see the whole class as a heatmap and can tell at a glance which concept needs fifteen more minutes on Monday.

3

Participation ≠ wealth

Grades reward session regularity, market activity, event participation, and demonstrated understanding — never coin balance. The student who got rich cornering wool and the student who went bankrupt learning why are graded on the same evidence: what they understood.

4

Integrity by design

Dozens of low-stakes checks make the return on cheating terrible: there's no single exam worth outsourcing. The anomaly engine also watches for multi-accounting, collusion, and suspicious wealth transfers — the same machinery that detects cartels.

Fair questions

What colleagues ask first

How much of my time does this really take?

Target: under 30 minutes a week. The dashboard is a five-minute read; detected moments arrive deduplicated with one-click responses; the weekly Lecture Playbook writes itself from your class's data. The seven-week arc runs on autopilot unless you reach for a lever.

Can students cheat?

The economics is multiplayer — there is no answer key to copy. Checks are frequent and low-stakes, so the ROI on cheating any single one is terrible by design. Grades reward engagement and demonstrated understanding, never wealth, and the anomaly system watches for multi-accounting and suspicious transfers.

What if some students just won't play?

Asynchronous-first: 20–60 minutes a few times a week, no scheduled sessions, and 48 hours offline can't ruin anyone (soft bankruptcy is a fresh-start loan). Disengagement shows up on your dashboard before it becomes a grade problem — and the daily puzzle, streaks, and fishing exist precisely to make logging in the path of least resistance.

How do grades get to my LMS?

Participation + mastery, weights you control, CSV export today; the grade model is LTI-1.3-shaped and LTI delivery is the first fast-follow.

What does it cost students?

Nothing in the pilot. It pairs with the free OpenStax text (CC BY), runs in any browser including phones, and there is no real money anywhere in the game — coins are coins.

Does it work for a small section?

NPC liquidity auto-scales with roster size. Below ~12 students some phenomena (cartel formation, the fishery collapse) get an NPC assist so the story beats still land — you'll see a gentle "thin world" note rather than a broken week.
